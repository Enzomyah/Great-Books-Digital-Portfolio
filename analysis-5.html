Analysis 05 Historical / Contextual Lens

Literary Archive  /  Analysis 05

The Song of Roland

By Anonymous

Literary Lens

The Historical / Contextual Lens looks at a literary work by studying the history, politics, religion, and military culture of the time it was written. Instead of looking at a story as just fantasy, this lens asks how real-world events and political goals influenced the author's message.

This lens is especially important for The Song of Roland (La Chanson de Roland) because the poem changes a real historical event to fit medieval politics. While the poem is based on a real skirmish that happened in the year 778 CE, it was not actually written down until about three hundred years later, around the late 1000s. Looking through this historical lens, we can see that the poem was used as motivational propaganda during the First Crusade, turning a small mountain ambush into an epic holy war between Christian France and Muslim Spain.

Background and Context

The Song of Roland is the oldest and most famous French "song of heroic deeds" (called a chanson de geste). Written anonymously in Old French verse, the most famous copy is found in the Oxford manuscript, which dates back to roughly 1100–1125 CE.

The real historical event happened in 778 CE. Emperor Charlemagne's army was leaving northern Spain after a military campaign, and his rearguard was ambushed in the mountain pass of Roncevaux by Christian Basque mountaineers. But by the late 1000s, when Pope Urban II called for the First Crusade (1095 CE), medieval storytellers changed the Basque attackers into a massive Muslim army. This turned a messy historical loss into an inspiring legend about loyalty, bravery, and dying for one's faith.

Summary

After fighting for seven long years in Spain, Emperor Charlemagne has conquered almost every city except Saragossa, ruled by the Muslim king Marsile. Realizing he cannot win in open combat, King Marsile sends an offer to surrender, promise tribute, and convert to Christianity if Charlemagne takes his army back to France. Charlemagne's brave nephew, Count Roland, distrusts the offer and wants to keep fighting, while Roland's stepfather, Count Ganelon, argues for peace. When Roland suggests that Ganelon go to Saragossa to deliver the final treaty, Ganelon thinks Roland is trying to get him killed and swears revenge. In Saragossa, Ganelon commits treason: he makes a secret deal with Marsile to ambush the back of Charlemagne's army, which will be led by Roland at the mountain pass of Roncevaux.

As the main French army marches home, a huge enemy army surrounds Roland and his 20,000 soldiers in the narrow mountain pass. Roland's wise best friend, Count Oliver, repeatedly begs Roland to blow his horn (the olifant) so Charlemagne can hear it and turn around to help. But Roland stubbornly refuses out of personal pride, fearing it will make him look cowardly. The French soldiers fight bravely and defeat thousands, but they are slowly wiped out. Only when almost all of his men are dead does Roland finally blow the horn so hard that his temples burst. Charlemagne hears the call, realizes what happened, arrests Ganelon, and races back. Although he arrives too late to save Roland, Charlemagne defeats the enemy army and puts Ganelon on trial for treason.

Main Themes and Literary Elements

Several major themes stand out in The Song of Roland:

  • Loyalty vs. Betrayal: In medieval feudal society, loyalty to your king and comrades was the most sacred duty. Roland's complete devotion to Charlemagne stands in stark contrast to Ganelon's selfish betrayal, showing that putting your personal grudges over the safety of your country is unforgivable.
  • Bravery (Prouesse) vs. Common Sense (Sapience): The poem contrasts two types of leadership through its famous quote: "Roland is brave and Oliver is wise." Oliver uses practical thinking and common sense, while Roland lets his stubborn pride get his men killed.
  • A Black-and-White World: The poem views the world in simple absolutes: "Christians are right and pagans are wrong." The enemy is shown as purely villainous to inspire medieval knights to join the Crusades.
  • Sacrifice and Honor: Roland dies facing the enemy, offering his glove up to God. In the medieval view, dying for your country and faith made you a martyr whose soul went straight to heaven.

Detailed Analysis

Using the Historical / Contextual Lens reveals how the poem reflected the beliefs, social rules, and military goals of eleventh-century France.

Reshaping History for the Crusades: Old historical records (like Einhard's biography, Life of Charlemagne) prove that the real attack in 778 CE was carried out by local Basque mountaineers defending their homeland. But three hundred years later, when Europe was encouraging knights to go fight in the Crusades, storytellers rewrote the story completely. They replaced the Basques with a giant Muslim army, portrayed Charlemagne as a 200-year-old holy defender of the faith, and turned an embarrassing military blunder into a legendary story of holy warriors.

The High Cost of Stubborn Pride: Looking at the warrior culture of the Middle Ages helps us understand Roland's actions. In his world, a knight's reputation and fear of shame were everything. When Oliver begged him to blow the horn for help, Roland refused because he worried people back in France would call him weak. His stubborn pride (which the French called demesure, or a lack of self-control) directly caused the death of twenty thousand brave men. The story quietly shows that courage without wisdom is dangerous.

Cultural and Modern Connections: The Song of Roland offers interesting parallels to Filipino and Bikolano cultural history:

  • The Ibalong Epic Tradition: The legendary heroism and defense of land in Roland echo the ancient Bikol epic Ibalong, where heroes like Baltog, Handiong, and Bantong defend their homeland and people against monstrous adversaries.
  • Communal Loyalty and Leadership: Just as the Franks valued loyalty to Charlemagne, Bikolano communities have historically held deep respect for communal leaders and solidarity during hardship.
  • Faith and Devotion: The religious zeal in the medieval epic can be compared with the passionate spiritual devotion of Bikolanos to Our Lady of Peñafrancia, though Bikolano devotion emphasizes maternal love, mercy, and communal unity rather than holy warfare.

Globally, the epic connects with United Nations SDG 16 (Peace, Justice, and Strong Institutions) by illustrating how political deception, propaganda that creates an "us-versus-them" mentality, and leadership driven by personal revenge undermine peace and lead to devastating conflicts.

Personal Insights and Lessons

For me, The Song of Roland shows how a hero's stubborn pride and a bitter betrayal can lead to tragedy during a holy war. Watching Roland refuse to call for help until it is far too late teaches that letting ego and personal grudges overrule common sense can have devastating, irreversible consequences for everyone involved.

Furthermore, looking into the background of the epic highlights how old stories can reshape history to present complex human conflicts as absolute struggles between good and evil. Recognizing how historical narratives are retold to serve political or ideological agendas reminds us to think critically about the stories we inherit and the real human cost of stubborn pride.
