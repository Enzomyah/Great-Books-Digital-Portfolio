Analysis 06 Biographical Lens

Literary Archive  /  Analysis 06

The Tale of Genji

By Murasaki Shikibu

Literary Lens

The Biographical Lens approaches literature by examining the author's life, personal experiences, education, and social world. Rather than analyzing a text entirely in isolation, this lens investigates how the author's real-world observations, private relationships, and emotional struggles are woven into the themes, characters, and psychological depth of the work.

Using the Biographical Lens on The Tale of Genji is illuminating because the work is inseparable from the life of Murasaki Shikibu. A lady-in-waiting in the imperial court serving Empress Shōshi (Akiko), Murasaki was unusually educated in classical Chinese literature, a privilege typically reserved for men. Her surviving personal diary, The Diary of Lady Murasaki (Murasaki Shikibu Nikki), reveals an introspective, keenly observant court woman navigating palace intrigue, social rivalries, and the isolation of cloistered court life. Through this biographical context, we understand that Genji’s psychological realism, poetic exchanges (waka), and the poignant sorrow of transience (mono no aware) reflect Murasaki's own lived insights into the beauty and fragility of eleventh-century Heian society.

Background and Context

The Tale of Genji (Genji Monogatari) was written during Japan's peaceful Heian period (around 1000–1012 CE) in the imperial capital of Kyoto. The author, Murasaki Shikibu, was a lady-in-waiting in the imperial court. She was well-educated and wrote her massive 54-chapter novel in kana, a Japanese phonetic script that noblewomen used for letters and stories.

In Heian court society, wealthy men were allowed to have multiple wives and romantic partners. A woman's comfort and status depended heavily on whether she had a powerful father to protect her and whether she gave birth to a male heir. Court life was obsessed with art, poetry, and beauty, but it was also shaped by a Buddhist belief called mono no aware - a deep, gentle sadness about the fact that youth, beauty, and worldly success never last forever.

Summary

The novel follows the life, romances, and court career of Hikaru Genji ("The Shining Prince"), the handsome and talented son of an emperor and a low-ranking concubine. To protect him from jealous palace rivals, the emperor takes Genji out of the line of succession and gives him the commoner surname Minamoto. Growing up, Genji carries a deep emotional longing for his mother, who died when he was a toddler. This leads him into a forbidden affair with his stepmother, Lady Fujitsubo, who gives birth to a secret son who eventually becomes emperor.

Later, Genji takes in Fujitsubo's young niece, Murasaki, raising her to be his ideal companion and the true love of his life. After spending some time in voluntary political exile by the coast due to court scandals, Genji returns to the capital and rises to the very top of the government, building a huge mansion with four seasonal gardens for the women in his life. But his later years are filled with sorrow. Lady Murasaki suffers quietly when Genji is pressured into an official political marriage with a young princess. After Murasaki passes away, Genji is left heartbroken and steps away from public life. The final chapters follow the next generation (two young courtiers named Kaoru and Niou) and their tragic pursuit of secluded princesses in the countryside of Uji.

Main Themes and Literary Elements

Several major themes give The Tale of Genji its rich emotional depth:

  • The Fleeting Nature of Life (Mono no Aware): The biggest theme in the book is that everything in life (youth, beauty, romantic highs, and political glory) fades away just like falling cherry blossoms.
  • The Hard Lives of Court Women: The novel shows how painful it was for noblewomen who had to wait alone behind screens for visits from their lovers, always worrying that they would be abandoned for someone younger or wealthier.
  • Poetry as Everyday Communication: Characters constantly exchange 31-syllable waka poems to flirt, apologize, or express private grief that was considered too intense to say in plain speech.
  • What Goes Around Comes Around (Karma): The choices characters make have moral consequences. For example, Genji is crushed when his young official wife is seduced by another man, which mirrors the secret betrayal Genji committed against his own father years before.

Detailed Analysis

Looking at The Tale of Genji through the Biographical Lens reveals how Murasaki Shikibu’s own lived experience, education, and sharp observations as a Heian court lady-in-waiting directly informed the novel's psychological depth and social atmosphere.

The Ghost of Lady Rokujō: One of the most famous parts of the book involves the living spirit of Lady Rokujō. She is highly educated, proud, and from a royal family, but Genji loses interest in her and leaves her alone. In a court where women were never allowed to show open anger, her bottle-up hurt and humiliation take the form of a wandering spirit (an ikiryō) that attacks Genji's pregnant wife. Feminist critics point out that this supernatural possession is the only way a silenced noblewoman's deep rage could break through and disrupt Genji's comfortable world.

Lady Murasaki's Hidden Sorrow: Murasaki is taken in as a child and shaped to be Genji's perfect partner. Even though she runs his home and is the person he loves most, she never gets the formal title of legal principal wife because she does not have rich, powerful relatives to back her up. When Genji marries a young royal princess just for political status, Murasaki has to act polite and unbothered in public. Murasaki Shikibu makes sure to describe Murasaki's private tears and depression, showing that even the most favored woman in the kingdom had very little real control over her own life.

Cultural and Modern Connections: The Tale of Genji connects meaningfully to contemporary themes and Filipino/Bikolano culture:

  • Poetry, Song, and Indirect Expression: The Heian practice of expressing feelings through subtle waka poetry carries parallels to traditional Bikolano and Filipino courtship arts (such as harana and poetic verses like tula or balitaw), where emotions and romantic feelings were communicated through metaphor, song, and refinement rather than direct speech.
  • Family Honor and Reputation: The deep concern over family status and avoiding public shame (chihaji) in the Heian court mirrors traditional Filipino values concerning family pride and social standing, showing that the tension between personal happiness and family obligations is a shared human experience across cultures.

Globally, the novel links directly to United Nations SDG 5 (Gender Equality) by exposing how women’s autonomy, financial security, and legal rights were subordinated to patriarchal power and marriage politics. It also connects with SDG 10 (Reduced Inequalities) by illuminating sharp disparities in social class, privilege, and the vulnerability of women who lacked powerful political patrons.

Personal Insights and Lessons

For me, The Tale of Genji, written by Murasaki Shikibu in eleventh-century Japan, explores the complex romantic and political life of Hikaru Genji, an aristocratic prince. Set within the refined Heian court, where poetry, art, and social rank shaped daily life, it examines the tension between personal desires and strict social expectations.

Beyond its courtly beauty, the novel ultimately reflects on the fleeting nature of youth and beauty, showing that love, power, and status are temporary. Observing the quiet sorrows of the women who lived behind palace screens reminds us that social prestige and surface elegance cannot protect anyone from heartache or change. True wisdom comes from recognizing what is genuinely meaningful rather than chasing shallow, passing worldly success.
