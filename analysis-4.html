Analysis 04 Formalist / New Critical Lens

Literary Archive  /  Analysis 04

The Panchatantra

By Traditionally attributed to Vishnu Sharma

Literary Lens

The Formalist / New Critical Lens focuses directly on how a story is built, including its plot structure, literary devices, point of view, tone, and use of irony. Instead of looking outside the text at historical events or the author's personal life, Formalism looks closely at the writing itself and asks how the form of the story shapes its meaning.

This lens is perfect for The Panchatantra because the book is a masterclass in clever storytelling structure. It is famous for its "frame tale" design (like a story inside a story, or Russian nesting dolls). Characters in one story tell a fable to prove a point, and animals inside that fable tell another story. Looking through the Formalist lens, we can see how the mix of entertaining animal dialogues, short poems with life advice (subhashitas), and clever plot twists all work together to teach practical life lessons.

Background and Context

The Panchatantra is an ancient Indian collection of animal fables written in Sanskrit. It is traditionally credited to a wise scholar named Vishnu Sharma, who wrote it around 300 BCE to 300 CE. According to the opening story, a king had three lazy, rebellious sons who refused to study. The king asked Vishnu Sharma to educate them in niti (wise leadership, practical thinking, and street-smart decision making). Instead of boring them with dry lectures, Vishnu Sharma told engaging animal stories that sharpened their minds in just six months.

Over the centuries, The Panchatantra became one of the most widely translated and traveled books in world history. It was translated into Persian, Arabic (known as Kalila wa Dimna), Greek, Latin, and many European languages, influencing famous story collections like Aesop's fables and the tales of La Fontaine.

Summary

The word Panchatantra means "Five Books" or "Five Principles," divided into five main sections:

  • Book 1: The Loss of Friends (Mitra-bheda): Tells how a strong friendship between a lion king and a gentle bull is ruined by a jealous jackal who whispers lies into both of their ears.
  • Book 2: The Gaining of Friends (Mitra-labha): Shows how four very different animals (a crow, a mouse, a turtle, and a deer) become best friends and combine their unique skills to save each other from hunters and traps.
  • Book 3: Of Crows and Owls (Kakolukiyam): Focuses on clever strategies and diplomacy during a feud between crows and owls, showing how a wise crow pretends to be an exile to sneak into the enemy camp and win the fight.
  • Book 4: Loss of Gains (Labdhapranasam): Tells the story of a monkey and a crocodile. When the crocodile tries to kidnap the monkey so his wife can eat the monkey's heart, the quick-thinking monkey saves his own life by claiming he left his heart hanging back on a tree branch.
  • Book 5: Ill-Considered Action (Apariksitakarakam): Warns against jumping to conclusions. The most famous tale is about a woman who sees blood on her pet mongoose's mouth, assumes it harmed her baby, and kills it in a fit of rage, only to find her baby safe and a deadly snake killed by the loyal mongoose.

Main Themes and Literary Elements

The storytelling in The Panchatantra relies on several distinct literary techniques:

  • Stories Inside Stories (Frame Tale): Having characters tell stories to each other allows the author to look at a moral problem from several angles at the same time, making the lessons stick in the reader's memory.
  • Talking Animals (Anthropomorphism): Using lions, jackals, and hares instead of human kings and politicians makes the lessons easy to enjoy and understand without getting bogged down in real-world political arguments.
  • Short Verses with Big Lessons: The prose story is regularly paused by short, memorable poems that sum up wise rules about human behavior, trust, and common sense.
  • Quick Wit Over Physical Might: In almost every fable, smaller and weaker animals (like the clever hare, the tiny mouse, or the monkey) defeat big, scary predators through patience, calm thinking, and smart tricks.

Detailed Analysis

Looking at The Panchatantra through the Formalist / New Critical Lens helps us see how its narrative design makes its life lessons so effective.

The Structure of Nested Stories: The way the stories are layered works like an interactive puzzle. In Book 1, when the jackal tries to turn the lion against the bull, he does not just give an opinion; he tells a story about someone who was tricked. The characters in that story then tell another fable to make their point. This clever setup trains the reader (and the young princes) to think critically about rumors and reports, teaching us to look at evidence carefully before believing gossip.

Dramatic Irony in The Loss of Friends: The sad breakup of the lion and the bull is built on dramatic irony. As readers, we know that both the lion and the bull are innocent and truly care about each other. But we watch in suspense as the sneaky jackal plays on their small fears with half-truths. It is a powerful structural demonstration of how easily misunderstandings and unverified rumors can turn good friends into enemies.

Formal Mechanics in Books 4 and 5: The supplied class analysis highlights Books 4 and 5 as clear examples of how formal story mechanics deliver moral wisdom:

  • Book 4: Loss of Gains (Labdhapraṇāśam) - The Monkey and the Crocodile: The plot hinges on verbal wit and narrative reversal. When the crocodile reveals his betrayal in the middle of the river, the monkey does not panic. Through quick-witted dialogue, he invents the claim that his heart is hanging on a fig tree, reversing the power dynamic and safely reaching shore.
  • Book 5: Ill-Considered Actions (Aparīkṣitakārakaṃ) - The Brahmin's Wife and the Mongoose: The story's formal structure uses rapid pacing, sensory contrast (the bloody snout versus the unharmed infant), and sudden dramatic shock. By placing the realization immediately after the violent action, the fable delivers an unforgettable lesson on the fatal danger of jumping to conclusions without checking evidence.

Cultural and Modern Connections: These fables connect directly with Bikolano and Filipino oral traditions:

  • Fables and Trickster Tales: The monkey's quick thinking in Book 4 shares strong storytelling roots with the popular Filipino fable The Monkey and the Turtle (or the Bikolano An Toraok asin an Bagon) and the folk figure Juan Pusong, where smaller creatures survive through wit rather than strength.
  • Avoiding Padalos-dalos Actions: The tragic mistake in Book 5 reinforces the Bikolano and Filipino moral warning against being padalos-dalos - acting recklessly or impulsively in anger without verifying the truth first.

Globally, these fables link directly to United Nations SDG 16 (Peace, Justice, and Strong Institutions). Just as the Brahmin's wife acted on false assumptions, societies and institutions must base judgments on verified evidence and due process rather than rumors, false accusations, or rushed reactions, in order to uphold justice and fairness.

Personal Insights and Lessons

For me, The Panchatantra is a collection of animal fables designed to teach smart life choices. Beyond their entertaining surface stories, the fables show that staying calm and using quick wit can help people escape the most dangerous situations.

The clever animals demonstrate that intellectual agility and composure under pressure are far more reliable than panic or brute strength. Most importantly, stories like the loyal mongoose teach that acting out of blind anger or jumping to conclusions without checking the facts can lead to painful, irreversible mistakes. Taking a moment to assess reality before reacting is a vital lesson in avoiding misunderstandings in our relationships and daily decisions.
