Analysis 01 Archetypal / Mythological Lens

Literary Archive  /  Analysis 01

The Odyssey

By Homer

Literary Lens

The Archetypal / Mythological Lens looks at literature through familiar symbols, character types, and story patterns that appear across different cultures and eras. It suggests that human stories share common building blocks because humans throughout history have faced similar fears, dreams, and challenges. Important character types in this lens include the Hero, the wise Mentor, the clever Trickster, and the dangerous Villain or Shadow. Common story patterns include the Hero's Journey, the Quest, the Fall, and symbolic Death and Rebirth.

This lens fits Homer's The Odyssey perfectly because it is one of the oldest and most famous examples of the hero's journey ever written. Instead of seeing Odysseus's voyage as just an exciting adventure across the sea, the archetypal lens helps us see that every island, monster, and challenge is a test of his character. His ten-year voyage home becomes a universal story about growing up, facing weaknesses, and fighting to rebuild one's life and family.

Background and Context

The Odyssey is an ancient Greek epic poem traditionally attributed to Homer. It was created near the end of the eighth century BCE (around 750–700 BCE). For a long time, the story was told out loud by traveling poets (called rhapsodes) before it was finally written down in Greek poetry.

The story takes place right after the Trojan War, the famous ten-year war told in Homer's other epic, the Iliad. But while the Iliad is mostly about soldiers fighting on the battlefield, The Odyssey focuses on the long, painful journey home (called nostos) and the struggle to survive. In ancient Greece, this story taught values that people cared deeply about: showing hospitality to travelers and strangers (xenia), respecting the gods, being loyal to your family and household (oikos), and keeping society peaceful and civilized.

Summary

After the fall of Troy, Odysseus, the king of Ithaca, sets sail for home with his fleet of twelve ships. But his journey turns into a ten-year nightmare when he angers Poseidon, the god of the sea, by blinding Poseidon's giant son, the Cyclops Polyphemus. As they try to cross the Mediterranean, Odysseus and his crew face one danger after another: the sweet lotus plant that makes sailors forget their homes, giant cannibals called Laestrygonians, the witch Circe who turns men into pigs, the deadly singing Sirens, the sea monsters Scylla and Charybdis, and the sacred cattle of the sun god Helios, which his hungry men foolishly eat, leading to their deaths.

Odysseus ends up trapped for seven years on the isolated island of Ogygia by the nymph Calypso. Finally, the gods Athena and Hermes step in to help him leave. After a final storm destroys his raft, he washes ashore in Phaeacia, where the kind king Alcinous listens to his story and gives him a ship home to Ithaca. Arriving home in secret disguised as an old beggar, Odysseus meets his loyal son Telemachus and his trusted swineherd Eumaeus. He finds his palace overrun by rude suitors who are wasting his food and trying to force Queen Penelope to marry one of them. During Penelope's archery contest, Odysseus strings his old hunting bow, defeats the suitors, reunites with his faithful wife and elderly father, and brings peace back to his kingdom.

Main Themes and Literary Elements

Several major themes and storytelling elements give The Odyssey its lasting impact:

  • Homecoming (Nostos) and Reclaiming Identity: The main goal of the story is not collecting treasure, but getting back home to family. Odysseus goes from being a lost, nameless wanderer ("Nobody") back to being a father, husband, and king.
  • Brains Over Brawn (Metis): Unlike Achilles, who relied mostly on sheer strength and fighting fury, Odysseus wins by thinking ahead, staying patient, and using clever tricks. Escaping the Cyclops and sneaking into his own palace in disguise prove that a sharp mind is often more effective than muscle.
  • Resisting Temptation: Odysseus and his crew are constantly tempted to give up their goal, whether by eating the lotus flower, lingering in Circe's palace, or accepting Calypso's promise of eternal youth. Odysseus chooses real human life, with all its aging and pain, over an easy, empty paradise.
  • Loyalty and Betrayal: Penelope waiting twenty years while tricking the suitors with her weaving, Telemachus standing up for his father, and the loyal old dog Argos waiting until Odysseus returns all stand out against the disloyal suitors and dishonest servants.
  • Hospitality (Xenia): In ancient Greece, welcoming strangers was a sacred duty. Good hosts like Nestor and the Phaeacians treat visitors with warmth, while monsters like the Cyclops and the rude suitors break this rule and face punishment.

Detailed Analysis

Looking at The Odyssey through the Archetypal / Mythological Lens, we can clearly see the classic stages of the Hero's Journey: leaving the ordinary world, facing dangerous trials, visiting the underworld, and returning home transformed.

The Hero and the Shadow: Odysseus is a classic Hero archetype, but he is far from perfect. His biggest flaw is his pride (hubris). For instance, after outsmarting the Cyclops Polyphemus, he cannot resist shouting his real name from his ship just to brag. That single boast brings Poseidon's curse down on his crew. To grow as a hero, Odysseus has to learn humility and patience. By the time he reaches Ithaca, he is willing to wear dirty rags and get insulted as a beggar in his own home before making his move. Meanwhile, Poseidon and the greedy suitors act as the Shadow / Villain archetype, representing chaos, selfishness, and disorder that threaten peace and community.

The Mentor: The goddess Athena is the classic Mentor archetype. Instead of waving a magic wand to solve every problem, Athena gives advice, gives Odysseus and Telemachus confidence, and guides them to make smart choices. Her wisdom represents the practical judgment and clear thinking that Odysseus needs to rely on.

Trials and Thresholds: Odysseus's wanderings are full of symbolic tests:

  • The Lotus-Eaters and the Sirens: These test self-control. They represent the danger of forgetting your purpose in life just to chase quick comfort or sweet illusions.
  • Circe and Calypso: Both are powerful enchantresses who test Odysseus in different ways. Circe turns men into wild beasts, showing how easily people can lose their human dignity, while Calypso offers him an easy life without death, testing whether he truly cares about his family and mortal life.
  • Descent into the Underworld: In Book 11, Odysseus journeys into the land of the dead. Speaking with the blind prophet Tiresias, his fallen war friends, and his late mother, Odysseus faces the reality of death, learns humility, and gains the guidance he needs to make it home.

The Final Test and Rebirth: Returning to Ithaca marks the final step of the journey. The contest of the bow is symbolic: only the true king has the strength and skill to string the ancient bow. By taking back his home and reuniting with Penelope, Odysseus restores balance and peace to his family and kingdom.

Cultural and Modern Connections: Odysseus’s story connects naturally with Filipino and Bikolano cultural values:

  • Pag-asam sa pag-uli: The longing for home and deep attachment to one's family and homeland mirrors the ancient Greek concept of nostos.
  • Pag-asikaso (Hospitality): The ancient duty of xenia (welcoming and feeding strangers before even asking their names) reflects warm Bikolano hospitality, where guests are treated with genuine respect and generosity.
  • Diskarte at Katatagan (Resilience and Wit): Odysseus’s sharp intelligence and ability to adapt to danger resemble the Filipino value of diskarte (finding creative, practical ways to survive) difficult trials.

On a global level, The Odyssey connects with United Nations Sustainable Development Goal 16 (Peace, Justice, and Strong Institutions) through Odysseus’s struggle to bring order and fairness back to a community overrun by lawless suitors. It also connects with SDG 10 (Reduced Inequalities) by highlighting the vulnerability of displaced travelers, strangers, and returning wanderers seeking protection and dignity.

Personal Insights and Lessons

For me, The Odyssey is ultimately a story about a long, hard journey home and the relentless fight to protect what you love. Reading through Odysseus's twenty-year absence and his trials across treacherous seas shows that staying smart and patient can help anyone overcome seemingly insurmountable problems.

Rather than relying solely on brute power, Odysseus repeatedly demonstrates that calculated intelligence, emotional discipline, and enduring patience are the qualities that preserve life and restore purpose. Most profoundly, this epic reminds me that family and home are worth fighting for, no matter how much time passes or how grueling the obstacles along the way become. The loyalty shared between Odysseus, Penelope, and Telemachus illustrates that our deepest commitments give us the resilience needed to complete our own life journeys.
