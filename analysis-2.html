Analysis 02 Feminist Lens

Literary Archive  /  Analysis 02

The Ramayana

By Traditionally attributed to Valmiki

Literary Lens

The Feminist Lens examines how literature portrays gender roles, power dynamics between men and women, and the lived experiences of female characters within patriarchal societies. It investigates how women's voices, choices, and dignity are represented or silenced, and critiques traditional standards that prescribe female obedience, sacrifice, and submission.

Applying the Feminist Lens to The Ramayana offers profound insight into the epic's female figures, most notably Sita. While traditional interpretations often hold Sita up merely as the ideal, submissive wife (pativrata), a feminist reading reveals her remarkable moral agency, inner strength, and dignity. From voluntarily choosing forest exile to standing firm against Ravana's threats in Lanka, enduring the ordeal of fire (Agni Pariksha), and ultimately calling upon Mother Earth (Bhoomi Devi) to vindicate her integrity, Sita's journey challenges simplistic patriarchal expectations. Examining secondary female characters such as Queen Kaikeyi and Surpanakha further exposes how ancient societal norms rewarded self-sacrifice while penalizing female assertion and autonomy.

Background and Context

The Ramayana is one of the two great ancient Sanskrit epics of India. It is traditionally attributed to the sage-poet Valmiki, who is honored in India as the Adi Kavi (the "First Poet"). Although the story was passed down by word of mouth for generations, scholars generally agree that the main written Sanskrit text took shape between the 5th and 2nd centuries BCE.

Historically, the epic was composed during a time when kingdoms were growing across northern India and classical Hindu traditions were being organized. The epic was not just an entertaining story; it was a guide for everyday life. It taught people what ideal behavior looked like: how a good son, brother, wife, and ruler should treat one another in society.

Summary

In the peaceful kingdom of Ayodhya, Prince Rama, the eldest son of King Dasharatha, is loved by everyone for his kindness, bravery, and honesty. After winning the hand of Princess Sita by stringing a divine bow, Rama is chosen to become the next king. But right before he is crowned, Queen Kaikeyi (misled by her jealous servant Manthara) demands that the king keep an old promise: make her son Bharata king instead, and banish Rama to the forest for fourteen years. Wanting to protect his father's honor and keep his father's word, Rama agrees without complaint. His wife Sita and his loyal brother Lakshmana refuse to leave his side and go with him into the wild.

While living peacefully in the forest, the brothers protect holy sages from demons. This angers the ten-headed demon king of Lanka, Ravana. Using a trick with a magical golden deer to lure Rama and Lakshmana away, Ravana kidnaps Sita and flies her to his island fortress, trying to force her to marry him. Sita bravely refuses. To rescue her, Rama teams up with Sugriva and an army of monkey warriors, led by the loyal Hanuman. Hanuman leaps across the ocean to find Sita and gives her Rama's ring as a sign of hope. Rama and his allies build a bridge to Lanka, defeat Ravana in a fierce battle, and rescue Sita. After his fourteen years of exile end, Rama returns to Ayodhya to be crowned king, beginning a peaceful golden age.

Main Themes and Literary Elements

Several major themes give The Ramayana its timeless moral value:

  • Doing What Is Right (Dharma): The heart of the story is living up to your moral duties. Rama chooses to keep his father's promise over taking the royal crown, showing that doing the right thing matters more than personal comfort or ambition.
  • Family Loyalty and Devotion: Every major relationship is built on deep loyalty: Lakshmana choosing to suffer in the forest with his brother, Bharata refusing to take the throne for himself, Sita standing by Rama through hardship, and Hanuman's selfless service (bhakti).
  • The Danger of Pride and Selfishness: Ravana is powerful, smart, and wealthy, but his huge ego and inability to control his desires cause his ruin. The story shows that power without good morals always ends in disaster.
  • Good vs. Evil: The war between Ayodhya and Lanka is an allegory for the constant struggle between harmony and goodness on one side, and cruelty, pride, and disorder on the other.

Detailed Analysis

When we examine The Ramayana through the Feminist Lens, we see how the epic reflects and critiques the lived realities, moral agency, and societal constraints of women in ancient India.

The Ideal Ruler (Maryada Purushottama): In ancient India, kings were not supposed to act like selfish tyrants; they were expected to serve their people and follow moral law. Rama represents the perfect leader because he puts the rules of truth and honor above his own feelings. When King Dasharatha cries about sending him away, Rama still leaves for the forest because if a king breaks his word, people lose trust in justice and truth (satya). Rama's actions were meant to teach ancient princes that true leadership comes from honesty and self-sacrifice.

Family Ties and Respect: Ancient Indian society placed great importance on large, united families where relatives supported each other. The contrast between Ayodhya and Lanka highlights this. In Ayodhya, the brothers love and respect one another; when Bharata hears about Rama's exile, he refuses to wear the crown and places Rama's sandals on the throne instead. In contrast, Lanka falls apart because Ravana is too proud to listen to his good brother Vibhishana, even banishing him for speaking the truth. The story warns that when families let pride and greed divide them, their power crumbles.

Cultural and Modern Connections: The Ramayana shares meaningful connections with Filipino and Bikolano cultural values:

  • Family Loyalty and Respect: Rama’s deep obedience to his father and the unconditional support among siblings reflect the strong family closeness cherished in Bikolano households.
  • Bayanihan and Cooperation: The collective effort of Rama, Lakshmana, Sugriva, Hanuman, and the Vanara army to rescue Sita mirrors the Filipino spirit of bayanihan, coming together as a community to help someone in distress.
  • Devotion and Faith: The epic’s theme of heartfelt devotion (bhakti) can be respectfully compared to Bikolano Marian devotion to Our Lady of Peñafrancia (Ina), while recognizing that the two traditions arise from distinct religious and historical contexts.
  • Heroism in Local Lore: Rama’s courage in defending the innocent from monsters carries parallels to warrior-heroes in the Bikol epic Ibalong (such as Baltog, Handiong, and Bantong), though each epic retains its unique cultural identity.

Globally, the epic connects with United Nations SDG 5 (Gender Equality) when examining Sita’s challenges and voice within ancient patriarchal expectations, and SDG 16 (Peace, Justice, and Strong Institutions) through Rama’s pursuit of ethical governance, lawfulness, and protection of the innocent.

Personal Insights and Lessons

For me, The Ramayana is an epic adventure about a prince who goes to great lengths to rescue his kidnapped wife. Beyond its thrilling battles and mythical journeys, it shows that doing what is right and staying loyal to your family matter most of all.

Rama's unhesitating willingness to honor his family obligations, alongside the deep devotion shown by Sita and Lakshmana, proves that integrity and loyalty are the true foundations of honor. At the same time, the epic provides a clear warning through Ravana: allowing pride and selfishness to take over can lead to an inevitable downfall, regardless of how much power or talent one possesses. Choosing righteousness over ego remains a timeless lesson for our daily lives.
