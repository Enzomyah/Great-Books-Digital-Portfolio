Analysis 03 Psychoanalytic Lens

Literary Archive  /  Analysis 03

The Mahabharata

By Traditionally attributed to Vyasa

Literary Lens

The Psychoanalytic Lens looks at literature through the minds, hidden feelings, and personal struggles of the characters. Rooted in psychology, it explores concepts like the selfish urges of the id, the realistic thinking of the ego, and the moral conscience of the superego. It also looks at how people use mental defense mechanisms like jealousy, guilt, and denial to hide their insecurities or past hurt.

This lens is a great fit for The Mahabharata because the story is really an intense study of family drama and human flaws. The characters are not simple comic-book heroes or villains; they are complicated people dealing with jealousy, anger, hurt pride, and difficult moral choices. By looking closely at characters like Duryodhana, Karna, and Arjuna, we can understand how hidden insecurities and wounded pride can cause an entire family and kingdom to tear each other apart.

Background and Context

The Mahabharata is one of the two massive Sanskrit epics of ancient India. It is traditionally credited to the sage Vyasa, who is also written into the story as an ancestor of the royal family. Written and expanded between about 400 BCE and 400 CE, it contains over 100,000 verses, making it one of the longest literary works ever created.

The story is set in northern India around the kingdoms of Hastinapura and Indraprastha. Right in the middle of the epic (in Book 6) is the famous philosophical poem, the Bhagavad Gita. In ancient Indian history, the epic reflected a time when warrior kingdoms were fighting for power. It deals deeply with big questions about human choices, karma (the law of cause and effect), and how hard it is to know the right thing to do during difficult, chaotic times.

Summary

The story follows a bitter rivalry between two sets of royal cousins: the five good Pandava brothers (led by Yudhishthira) and the hundred jealous Kaurava brothers, led by Prince Duryodhana. Even after the Pandavas take a dry, empty land and turn it into a wealthy city called Indraprastha, Duryodhana cannot stand seeing his cousins succeed. Along with his scheming uncle Shakuni, Duryodhana tricks Yudhishthira into a rigged game of dice. Unable to walk away from the challenge, Yudhishthira gambles away everything: his wealth, his kingdom, his brothers, himself, and even their wife Draupadi, who is treated disrespectfully in front of the royal court.

As punishment, the Pandavas are forced to live in the forest for twelve years, plus one final year in hiding. They follow all the rules of their exile, but when they return, Duryodhana stubbornly refuses to give them back even five small villages to keep the peace. War becomes the only option. On the battlefield of Kurukshetra, the great archer Arjuna breaks down in tears because he does not want to kill his own relatives, teachers, and friends. Lord Krishna then speaks to him (in the Bhagavad Gita), reminding him to do his duty without being attached to results. An eighteen-day war takes place, killing almost everyone on both sides. Even though the Pandavas technically win, their victory feels empty because they lost so many people they loved.

Main Themes and Literary Elements

Several powerful themes run through The Mahabharata:

  • The Poison of Jealousy: Duryodhana's refusal to be happy with what he has drives the entire war. The story shows how envy destroys peace of mind and ruins family bonds.
  • Insecurity and Identity: Characters like Karna (who was abandoned as a baby by his royal mother and raised by a chariot driver) carry deep emotional pain. Karna is so eager to prove himself that he stays loyal to Duryodhana, even when he knows Duryodhana is wrong.
  • The Complexity of Right and Wrong (Dharma): Doing the right thing is rarely simple in this epic. Honorable elders like Bhishma feel forced by oaths to fight for an evil prince, while the Pandavas and Krishna sometimes bend traditional battle rules to defeat bullies, showing that real life is full of tough moral choices.
  • The Real Cost of War: Winning a war does not erase grief or trauma. The heartbreaking cries of mothers who lost their sons show that violence leaves everyone with painful scars.

Detailed Analysis

Using the Psychoanalytic Lens helps us look beneath the surface actions of the characters to see what was really driving them emotionally.

Duryodhana's Deep Envy: Duryodhana is a clear example of someone whose ego is completely controlled by jealousy and low self-esteem. Growing up next to gifted cousins (Arjuna's amazing archery, Bhima's strength, and Yudhishthira's good reputation), Duryodhana felt like their success made him look small. His obsession with humiliating them at the dice game was not just about politics; it came from an unhealthy desire to crush the people who made him feel insecure. He convinces himself that his cousins are out to get him because he cannot admit his own flaws.

Karna's Need for Acceptance: Karna is one of the most tragic characters in the story. Because he was rejected by his birth mother, he grows up with a deep fear of being worthless and an extreme sensitivity to insults about his background. He clings to Duryodhana because Duryodhana was the first person to show him respect and give him a royal title. Even when Krishna tells him the truth about his royal family, Karna chooses to stay with Duryodhana, showing how childhood wounds can make people loyal to the wrong crowd just to feel valued.

Arjuna's Breakdown on the Battlefield: Right before the battle starts, Arjuna drops his weapons and shakes with anxiety. In psychological terms, this is a moment where his human empathy clashes directly with his social duty as a soldier. Facing the reality of killing his own family breaks him down emotionally. Krishna’s guidance in the Bhagavad Gita addresses this inner crisis of duty (dharma) and moral responsibility. The psychoanalytic lens helps readers explore these inner conflicts, motives, and unspoken fears as universal human experiences, without needing to label or diagnose characters with modern clinical disorders.

Personal Insights and Lessons

For me, The Mahabharata shows how wanting too much power, jealousy, and fighting can ruin an entire family. Watching the bitter conflict between two groups of cousins demonstrates how quickly shared blood and kinship can be poisoned when people become obsessed with status and envy.

Through the painful consequences of the war at Kurukshetra, the epic teaches that being fair, doing the right thing, and caring for others are infinitely better than simply trying to win at all costs. An obsession with victory achieved through malice leaves everyone wounded and empty. The story reminds us that kindness, honesty, and family unity are far more valuable than pride or power.
